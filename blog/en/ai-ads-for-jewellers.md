## Why Is Jewellery Hard for AI?

Jewellery is one of the harder things to ask a video model to draw. Four problems repeat.

**Stones.** A necklace has a count of stones. A model can add one, drop one or blur two together. A customer who owns a piece or has seen it in your store will notice.

**Settings and prongs.** Prongs, bezels and claws are small, fine and repetitive. They are the first thing to melt or merge when a piece moves in frame.

**Metal and reflections.** Gold and silver reflect their surroundings. As the camera moves, the reflections must change in a way that makes physical sense. When they do not, the metal looks like plastic.

**Product drift.** Over 30 seconds, the piece in frame 1 can slowly become a different piece by frame 200. Pendant shape, chain length or the design of a jhumka can shift without any single frame looking obviously wrong.

Models trained on jewellery imaging help. Your review matters more. For the detail on [why stones drift in AI jewellery video](/blog/en/ai-jewellery-video-accuracy.html), read the accuracy guide.

## How Should a Jeweller Check a Draft?

Put the real piece next to the video and compare. Pause the video at three or four points and check:

1. **Stone count.** Count the stones in the video against the piece. Do it for the centre stone and for the side stones.
2. **Setting.** Is it the same setting type, with the same number of prongs or the same bezel?
3. **Finish.** Is the metal the right colour and finish? Matte, polished, antique and rose gold all read differently.
4. **Shape and proportion.** Does the pendant, ring head or earring drop stay the same size and shape from the first second to the last?
5. **What the piece does.** Does a hinged piece hinge the way it does in real life? Does a clasp sit where it sits on the real piece?

If any of these fail, say which one and at what second. That is a precise revision request, and it is what the revision round in the plan is for.

A good rule: if your best salesperson would say "that's not our piece", it is not ready to post.

## What Are AI Ads Not Good For?

Here is where AI ads are the wrong tool.

**A piece that must match stock exactly.** If the ad shows a piece and a customer walks in asking for it, the piece they are shown must be one they can buy. For one-of-a-kind or made-to-order pieces, AI drift is a real risk. For a standard catalogue item you can check against stock, it is manageable.

**Real people and real moments.** A customer wearing the piece at her own wedding, your karigar at the bench, your family-run showroom's third generation. These are stories about real people. An AI ad cannot stand in for them, and it should not try.

**Fine detail you want to be the star.** A detailed polki set or a heavy kundan necklace with hundreds of stones is exactly where models are most likely to blur. If the detail is the sale, a macro shoot of the real piece does the job better.

**Anything that has to be literally true.** Weight, purity, hallmark, carat. These belong in your copy and your price tag, not in what a video model draws.

## Do I Need to Label AI-Generated Jewellery Ads?

Check the guideline, then decide your own line.

India's advertising self-regulator, the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI), published [Guidelines for Responsible Labelling of Synthetically Generated Content in Advertising](https://www.ascionline.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Guidelines-for-Responsible-Labelling-of-Synthetically-Generated-Content-in-Advertising.pdf). Here is what the document says, and only what it says.

- It defines synthetically generated content (SGC) as audio, visual or audio-visual content in an advertisement that is artificially or algorithmically created or materially altered to appear real, and that depicts a person or event in a way likely to be believed true.
- Labelling is mandatory where SGC "materially influences consumer decisions" and the lack of a label would mislead consumers. Its examples include synthetically generated influencers and ambassadors, and demonstrating a product that does not currently exist.
- No label is required for minor changes with no material impact on a consumer's ability to make an informed choice, such as colour correction, standard blemish removal, decorative backgrounds and obviously fantastical elements.
- Where a label is used, the guideline gives examples such as "Audio/Video created using AI" and "Audio/Video enhanced using AI", and allows other labels that accurately inform the consumer.
- It lists as prohibited, even with a label, ads that exaggerate product results or features through misleading visuals, and ads that use a person's likeness without consent.
- Every ad containing SGC remains subject to the full ASCI Code.

The guideline does not name jewellery, and it does not say a video of a real piece made with AI tools needs a label or does not. It asks whether the AI use materially influences the buyer's decision and whether leaving it out would mislead. That is a judgment about your ad.

This is ASCI's self-regulatory guidance. This page does not claim it is a legal requirement, and it is not legal advice.

A practical approach for a jeweller:

- **If the piece shown is your real piece, accurately drawn,** the AI use is closer to production method than to a claim about the product. Whether to disclose anyway is a brand decision.
- **If you add a generated model or generated setting,** think harder about whether it affects what the buyer believes about the piece or about who is wearing it.
- **If the video shows something that does not match what you sell,** no label fixes that. It is the misleading-visual problem the guideline treats as prohibited.

Pick one disclosure line and use it on every AI-made ad, for example "Video created using AI" in the caption or the end frame. Consistency is easier to defend than a case-by-case call.
