The problem

The forecast lives in a spreadsheet. The market doesn't.

Most plants buy on last month's average, a supplier's phone call, and the memory of whoever has been in the role longest. That works until the band moves fifteen percent in a quarter.

📉

Averages hide the risk

A single expected price tells you nothing about the downside. You need the range you're actually exposed to, and the odds attached to it.

⏳

Decisions arrive late

By the time the monthly review lands, the window closed. Buying calls are weekly problems being answered on a monthly clock.

🧾

Nobody can reconstruct why

Ask why 400 tonnes were bought in March and you get a story, not a record. Audit season turns into archaeology.

How it works

History in. A defensible call out.

No new ERP, no rip-and-replace. It reads what you already have and returns a decision your buyer can sign.

Step 01

Feed it what you have

Purchase and consumption history, inventory, open POs, lead times, and market price series. Files or a scheduled export, both work.

Step 02

Forecast and band

Weekly demand per material and plant, plus a P10/P50/P90 price band. It states the range honestly instead of pretending to one number.

Step 03

One play, one approver

Quantity, timing, supplier split, and the drivers behind them. A buyer approves, or overrides with a reason. Both are recorded.

The recommendation

Five plays. Never a maybe.

Every material resolves to exactly one, with the quantity and the timing attached. Ambiguity is what your team already has.

BUY NOW

Cover is short or the band is climbing. Commit the volume this week.

WAIT

Cover holds and the band favours patience. Do nothing, deliberately.

PARTIAL BUY

Take part of the volume now, leave the rest exposed to a softer band.

SPLIT SUPPLIERS

Same volume, rebalanced, so one supplier never owns your line.

HEDGE / LOCK

Flagged for the memo when a longer lock beats spot exposure.

Governance

It recommends. Your buyer decides.

No system should place an order on your behalf, and this one can't. Every recommendation waits for a named approver, keeps the reasoning that produced it, and stores the override when a human disagrees. Twelve months later you can still answer "why did we buy that".

1
Reasoning is structured, not narrated

Drivers are recorded as data. The same inputs give the same call, every run.

2
Approval is mandatory

Nothing moves without a buyer or approver on the record.

3
Overrides count as evidence

Disagreements are captured with a reason and reviewed against outcomes.
